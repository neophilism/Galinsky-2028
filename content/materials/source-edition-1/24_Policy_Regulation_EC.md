# Regulation EC Policy Action Kit

*Let charitable commitment—not wealth alone—set priority in a limited investment round.*

Explain the Complete the Revolution proposal accurately, include its safeguards and limits, answer predictable questions, and offer lawful volunteer actions.

> **For:** Issue ambassadors, personal-network organizers, digital ambassadors, hosts, callers, canvassers, and policy reviewers

> **Status:** Campaign proposal and volunteer guidance. Verify changing law and campaign instructions before regulated activity.

## The short version

> **One-sentence explanation:** Regulation EC would let investors compete for access to a limited offering by pledging a larger percentage of dividends and stock to a paired nonprofit, with offerings accepting investments of any size in pledge-ranked order.

> **Always say:** This is a campaign proposal, not a current program or personal entitlement. Legislation, public rulemaking, implementation safeguards, and often state or international participation would be required.

## The problem and the mechanism

Many private investment opportunities exclude ordinary investors, while nonprofits face donation cycles that fluctuate with attention. The proposal connects broader access with a permanent charitable ownership stake.

1. Create a specific SEC offering framework with a paired nonprofit and standardized disclosures.
2. Rank investor priority by the pledged percentage of dividends and stock, not by the dollar amount invested.
3. Require the issuer to accept investments of any size in descending pledge order until the round is full.
4. Transfer pledged dividends and stock through enforceable mechanisms that the investor cannot quietly evade.
5. Protect investors and nonprofits through suitability, loss, valuation, governance, conflict, resale, custody, tax, and anti-fraud rules.

## Safeguards that belong in the pitch

- Clear warning that private investments can lose all value and be illiquid
- Anti-evasion and related-party rules for company/nonprofit relationships
- No charitable tax-deduction promise; tax treatment requires law and individual advice
- Small-investor exposure limits, disclosure, cooling-off, cancellation, and complaint rights
- Nonprofit independence, mission lock, and transparent valuation of donated securities

## What not to claim

> **Accuracy boundary:** A volunteer earns trust by naming limits before an opponent has to.

- Do not describe any investment as safe, approved, guaranteed, or available.
- Do not recommend a security or collect investor information.
- Do not imply current accredited-investor or crowdfunding rules disappear automatically.
- Do not promise a tax deduction, return, allocation, or nonprofit outcome.

## Questions people will ask

| Question | Grounded answer |
| --- | --- |
| Can anyone invest? | That is the aim, subject to congressionally and SEC-defined eligibility, disclosure, and exposure limits. No Reg EC offering exists today. |
| Why rank by a percentage pledge? | It gives small investors a way to compete for priority based on charitable commitment rather than wealth. |
| Could insiders game the nonprofit? | Yes, unless law imposes independence, valuation, related-party, anti-evasion, custody, and reporting rules. |
| Is this crowdfunding? | It would be a new framework and must be compared with existing Regulation Crowdfunding and other exemptions. |

## A two-minute conversation

1. Ask what the person wants solved before naming the policy.
2. State the problem in one sentence without exaggerating it.
3. Give the one-sentence explanation and the two most relevant safeguards.
4. Name the main dependency or unresolved implementation question.
5. Ask: ‘What would you need to see tested before this became law?’
6. Offer the official platform and primary sources; do not demand agreement.

> **Close:** The value of this proposal is that it gives us something specific to test. The full campaign text is at galinsky2028.us, and I’d be interested in the strongest question you think the legislation must answer.

## Actions volunteers can take

1. Explain the mechanism without naming or recommending a company or security.
2. Invite securities lawyers, investor advocates, nonprofit governance experts, founders, and small investors to red-team the framework.
3. Compare current SEC accredited-investor and crowdfunding rules with the proposal.
4. Collect policy questions, never investment leads or financial profiles.
