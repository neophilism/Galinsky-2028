SECTION 3. FINDINGS.
The Congress finds the following:
(1) Free Speech and Fair Elections are Fundamental.
Free and fair elections are the foundation of the United States’ constitutional republic.
Robust political debate and honest elections are indispensable to self-government.
Protecting political expression, voter confidence, and electoral integrity is a paramount
federal interest.
(2) Surveillance Abuses Threaten Political Participation.
Surveillance powers designed for foreign intelligence have been misused in ways that could
chill Americans’ political activities. A declassified Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court
opinion in April 2022 detailed a “persistent and widespread” pattern of FBI agents
conducting improper queries on data collected under Section 702 of the Foreign Intelligence
Surveillance Act, including searches related to protestors, activists, and political donors.
While subsequent reforms have improved compliance (with a court finding over 98% query
compliance after new safeguards), Congress finds that no government agency should spy on
Americans’ politics without a strict legal basis, and that individuals whose political
campaigns or activities were unlawfully surveilled have too few remedies under current
law.
(3) Outside Funding of Election Places Undermines Trust.
After the 2020 election, it was revealed that private funds from non-governmental entities
were used to support certain local election offices, raising concerns about real or perceived
influence. Since then, at least 29 states — including Wisconsin (by constitutional
amendment in 2024) and Wyoming (by statute in 2025) — have prohibited or strictly
limited private funding for election administration. It is the sense of Congress that federal
law should establish uniform guardrails on the acceptance of private money by local
election officials or offices in connection with administration of federal elections, to prevent
distortive effects and ensure all jurisdictions have equal access to resources.
(4) Ballot Fraud Incidents Highlight Need for Deterrence.
Documented abuses in absentee ballot handling have undermined public trust in elections.
In Bridgeport, Connecticut’s 2023 mayoral primary, surveillance videos surfaced showing
individuals illicitly depositing multiple absentee ballots into drop boxes. A state court found
the evidence credible and took the extraordinary step of overturning the primary results
and ordering a new election. Subsequent investigation led to multiple arrests of political
operatives on charges of ballot fraud. Such misconduct, while not representative of the
broader election system, demonstrates a clear need for stronger federal deterrents and
remedies to protect “ballot-handling integrity” and ensure every legitimate vote is counted
as cast.

(5) Voting System Breaches Demand Federal Response.
The security of election infrastructure remains a critical concern. In Coffee County, Georgia,
following the 2020 election, unauthorized persons gained covert access to voting machines
and election servers, copying sensitive software and data in what has been described as
possibly the “biggest election breach” in U.S. history. Several individuals involved were
indicted at the state level, yet significant gaps in investigation and accountability remain.
Congress finds that additional federal tools are needed to address and prevent insider
threats or breaches of voting systems, without relitigating past outcomes.
(6) Government-Induced Censorship is a Serious Threat.
Government-induced censorship of lawful speech has emerged as a serious threat to First
Amendment freedoms. In Missouri v. Biden (2023), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth
Circuit found that federal officials — including White House and FBI personnel — “likely…
coerced” social media platforms into suppressing certain posts and viewpoints, and that
such pressure campaigns violated the First Amendment. Although the Supreme Court
ultimately vacated the district court’s injunction on procedural grounds, the factual record
and parallel congressional investigations have revealed a “censorship-by-proxy” problem.
(7) Only Congress Can Set Federal Candidate Qualifications.
The Constitution assigns Congress the responsibility to safeguard federal elections and the
political process. In Trump v. Anderson (2024), the Supreme Court unanimously held that
only Congress — and not the states — can enforce Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment
with respect to disqualifying individuals from federal office. That decision confirms that
attempts by State officials to unilaterally bar otherwise qualified candidates from the ballot
for federal office infringe upon a uniquely federal prerogative. Congress therefore finds that
federal law may establish uniform protections governing ballot access in federal elections,
consistent with constitutional and statutory processes.
(8) Protecting Democracy Requires Vigilance and Enforcement.
It is imperative that all branches and levels of government, as well as private actors,
uphold the integrity of the democratic process. Acts of intimidation, retaliation, fraud,
tampering, censorship, or sabotage that meaningfully burden a federal election corrode
public trust and suppress citizen participation.
(9) A Bipartisan Tradition of Protecting Free Expression and the Franchise.
Congress honors the legacies of Medgar Evers and Charlie Kirk, after whom this Act is
named, as individuals who, in different eras and from different political traditions, were
united by a shared commitment to free expression, democratic participation, and the right
of Americans to engage in the political process without fear or intimidation. Both were
victims of political violence aimed at silencing lawful political advocacy. Their names
symbolize this Act’s dedication to protecting the First Amendment, safeguarding the
integrity of elections, and rejecting the use of coercion or violence as a tool of political power.

SECTION 4. DEFINITIONS.
For purposes of this Act:
(1) Federal election.
“Federal election” means any election (general, special, primary, or runoff) held in whole or
in part to select or nominate an individual to serve as President or Vice President of the
United States, as a Presidential elector, or as a Member of the United States Senate or
House of Representatives. This includes elections for Delegate or Resident Commissioner to
Congress, and any caucus or convention with authority to nominate or select a candidate for
the foregoing offices. It does not include elections purely for state or local office, except
when such election is held concurrently with and on the same ballot as a federal election (in
which case this Act’s provisions apply to conduct impacting the federal portion of the
election).
(2) Candidate.
“Candidate” means, with respect to a federal election, an individual who has qualified
under state or federal law to be on the ballot (or to have write-in votes counted) for
nomination or election to federal office, or who has publicly declared their intent to seek
such office and has taken substantial steps toward campaign organization (such as filing
the required candidacy statement with the Federal Election Commission). “Political
committee” and “campaign” refer to any committee, party organization, fund, or entity
(whether incorporated or not) that is legally authorized under federal or state law to
support or oppose the election of one or more candidates for federal office, including
authorized candidate committees, political party committees, and independent expenditure
committees, as those terms are defined in the Federal Election Campaign Act.
(3) Political expression.
“Political expression” means speech, press, or expressive conduct by any person relating to a
political campaign, public issue, or matter of public policy or governance, which is protected
from government abridgment by the First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution. This
includes advocacy on election-related issues; commentary on officials, candidates, or
legislation; writing legislation; writing books with political content or underlying political
messages; creating protest music; creating other content related to politics or ideas affecting
the polity including creating images or works in any other expressive medium;
encouragement to vote or to refrain from voting; display of political signs or symbols;
peaceful rallies, protests, or other political association; the publication or online sharing of
writings and images about public affairs; and communications on social media concerning
public issues. Political expression is not limited to accurate or popular speech — even false,
unorthodox, or controversial statements about public issues are covered, so long as they do
not fall into a traditionally unprotected category (such as true threats, incitement of
imminent lawless action, criminal solicitation, or defamation of a private figure). For
avoidance of doubt, speech does not lose protection because it is anonymous, politically

(4) Prohibited Act.
“Prohibited Act” means any act or omission described as unlawful in Sections 101(a)(1)

(5) Materially burden.
“Materially burden” means to impose a significant, non-trivial impediment, distortion, or

person’s ability to participate in or speak about the election.
(6) Under color of law.
“Under color of law” means under the authority of any statute, ordinance, regulation, policy,
or custom of a state or of the United States, or under the pretense of such authority. Acts by
an official in their official capacity (or under the guise of official right) are acts under color
of law even if they exceed the official’s lawful authority.
(7) Knowingly or willfully.
“Knowingly” or “willfully” means that an action was undertaken with actual knowledge of
the facts giving rise to the violation and with intent to commit the act that constitutes the
violation.
(8) Election system.
“Election system” means any mechanical or electronic infrastructure used in the
administration of a federal election, including voting machines and tabulators, vote tally
software, election management systems, voter registration databases, electronic pollbooks,
ballot drop-boxes, or other related equipment and technology.
(9) Foreign national.
“Foreign national” has the meaning given in 52 U.S.C. § 30121(b).
(10) Democracy Defense and Remediation Trust Fund.
“Democracy Defense and Remediation Trust Fund” means the Treasury trust fund
established by Section 301 of this Act.
(11) Qualified public-interest plaintiff.
“Qualified public-interest plaintiff” means an organization that A) has been continuously
active for at least three years prior to filing suit and B) demonstrates organizational injury
or representational standing as described in Section 201(b)(2). An individual U.S. citizen
may not bring a public-interest action on behalf of the public or any definable group unless
acting as a duly authorized representative of a qualified public-interest plaintiff.

(12) Contractor.
“Contractor” means any person or private entity (including any subcontractor, vendor,
consultant, or agent) that provides services, goods, or other support to the United States or
any department, agency, or instrumentality thereof, whether pursuant to a contract, task
order, grant, memorandum of understanding, informal arrangement, or other form of
authorization.
(13) Acting under the direction, control, or authority of the United States.
“Acting under the direction, control, or authority of the United States” means performing
activities, functions, or tasks that were assigned, directed, supervised, authorized, financed,
or otherwise controlled by the United States (including by contract, tasking order,
memorandum, oral instruction, operational tasking, or other mode of direction), or where
the United States otherwise exercised de facto control over the person’s conduct. This term
includes, without limitation, circumstances in which the United States:
(A) provided or arranged payment, equipment, training, weapons, communications, or
other material support for the person’s activities;
(B) issued operational orders, tasking instructions, or supervision;
(C) integrated the person into a chain of command or otherwise exercised day-to-day
operational control;
(D) expressly or tacitly authorized, ratified, or acquiesced in the conduct; or
(E) recruited or otherwise caused a person to act on the United States’ behalf.
The presence of one or more of these indicia is sufficient but not necessary to establish that
a person acted under the direction, control, or authority of the United States.
(14) Coerce.
“Coerce” means an explicit or implicit communication by an official that a private entity
will suffer adverse governmental action (such as regulatory enforcement, loss of contracts,
or initiation of prosecution) unless the entity removes, labels, or restricts specified lawful
content, and where the official making the communication had actual knowledge that the
threatened action would likely be effectuated.
(15) Significant encouragement.
“Significant encouragement” means repeated, targeted, and unilateral communications by
an official that, in the totality of the circumstances, are reasonably likely to overbear the
independent judgment of a private entity. Isolated advisory, informational, or
non-threatening communications are not “significant encouragement.”

Title I — Protecting Elections and Political Speech
SEC. 101. Prohibited acts of election interference and political
intimidation.
(a) Prohibitions. —
It is unlawful for any person, whether acting under color of law or as a private individual,
to engage in any of the following Prohibited Acts, if committed with respect to a federal
election or in connection with political expression about a federal election, and done for the
purpose of interfering with, undermining, or retaliating against any person’s lawful
participation in a federal election or campaign, or for the purpose of influencing a federal
election outcome by wrongful means:
(1) Intimidation or retaliation against candidates, campaigns, or supporters. —
No person shall intimidate, threaten, coerce, harass, or punish any other person because of
their involvement in a federal election campaign. This includes intimidation or retaliation
against anyone for:
(A) being a candidate for federal office (or exploring or intending to become one);
(B) working for or donating to a candidate, political party, or political committee in
connection with a federal election; or
(C) engaging in lawful political expression in support of or opposition to any federal
candidate.
Prohibited conduct under this paragraph includes, but is not limited to:
(i) threats of physical harm, stalking, or property destruction directed at a candidate or
campaign staffer or volunteer;
(ii) threats of financial, legal, or regulatory harm (such as launching baseless
investigations, audits, or lawsuits; terminating someone’s employment; or canceling
business dealings) directly because of that person’s candidacy, campaign support, or
political donations;
(iii) “doxing” (publicizing personal information) or spreading malicious falsehoods about
an individual with the intent and reasonably foreseeable effect of provoking third-party
harassment or violence against them due to their political activities; or
(iv) any other action—whether under color of official authority or otherwise—that is
intended to deter a reasonable person from running for office, to cause them to withdraw
from an election, or to chill their freedom to speak, associate, or donate in the political
process, or to retaliate against them for having done so.
(Nothing in this paragraph shall be construed to criminalize constitutionally protected
political advocacy or criticism. This provision targets true threats, extortionate or coercive
behavior, and misuse of official power to target political opponents. It does not encompass

lawful and peaceful persuasion, negative campaigning, robust public criticism, boycotts, or
other routine forms of political opposition that do not involve unlawful threats or abuse of
power.)
(2) Ballot-handling fraud and corruption. —
No person shall engage in fraud or corruption in the casting, collection, counting, or
certification of ballots in a federal election. Prohibited acts include:
(A) casting or attempting to cast ballots on behalf of others without authorization (for
example, voting in the name of another person, or one person casting multiple ballots,
except where expressly permitted by law such as an authorized proxy for a disabled voter);
(B) counting, recording, or certifying votes that one knows are fictitious, invalid, or
unauthorized (e.g., knowingly tallying fraudulent ballots, or reporting more votes than
actual voters);
(C) intentionally failing to count or report lawful votes (e.g., discarding valid ballots, or
certifying an incomplete or false vote tally);
(D) altering, suppressing, or falsifying any election return, tally sheet, certificate of
election, or other official document to reflect anything other than the true votes cast;
(E) destroying, defacing, hiding, or spoiling any cast ballot or significant election record,
except as permitted by law (such as routine destruction of ballots after the legally required
retention period);
(F) knowingly accepting or counting ballots or votes that are materially fraudulent or not
legally eligible (for instance, counting the vote of a deceased or non-eligible person, unless
pursuant to a court order); or
(G) organizing or participating in an illegitimate ballot harvesting scheme that violates
applicable law (for example, collecting absentee ballots from voters and failing to deliver
them, or deceiving voters into handing over ballots which are then altered or not
submitted).
(This paragraph is intended to supplement, not supplant, existing federal and state
prohibitions on election fraud. It shall be enforced consistently with those laws. It does not
reach unintentional errors or minor procedural irregularities that do not amount to knowing
fraud or alteration of true vote counts. Lawful assistance to voters where permitted by
statute, and good-faith judgments by election officials interpreting ambiguous voter
markings, are not prohibited. Only deliberate acts to inject false votes, eliminate true votes,
or corrupt the counting process fall within this ban.)
(3) Unauthorized tampering with election systems or equipment. —
No person shall, without lawful authorization, access or tamper with election systems or
equipment used in a federal election, or the data they contain. This includes both physical
and electronic tampering. Prohibited acts include:

(A) gaining unauthorized entry into a secured area housing ballots or voting machines;
(B) breaking seals, altering settings, or interfering with the calibration or configuration
of voting machines, tabulators, or other election devices contrary to law or without
permission;
(C) hacking into, introducing malware to, or exploiting vulnerabilities in any
election-related software, firmware, or communication network;
(D) initiating any unauthorized intrusion into an election management network or
database (such as a voter registration database), or exceeding one’s authorized access by
using valid credentials for improper purposes;
(E) altering or deleting electronic election data (including vote totals, ballot images, audit
logs, or voter records) without legal approval; or
(F) willfully failing to perform a duty of cybersecurity or physical security required by
federal or state law (or by one’s official position), if such failure enables another person to
gain unauthorized access to, or to undetectably alter, any part of an election system.
(For purposes of this paragraph, “without authorization” includes acts by an election official
or contractor that are materially outside the scope of their lawful duties—e.g., an IT
administrator using their credentials to make unsanctioned software changes for improper
purposes. This paragraph shall not be construed to penalize legitimate, non-deceptive
security research or testing of election systems that is conducted with the knowledge and
permission of the relevant election authority. Conversely, an individual who abuses otherwise
authorized access with wrongful intent is covered just as an external hacker would be. As an
affirmative defense, a defendant may show that they accessed an election system solely to
expose vulnerabilities to the responsible officials, and not to alter results or data, and that
they acted without malice, promptly reported the issue, and did not unreasonably risk the
integrity of any election. If proven, the court may deem such conduct outside the scope of this
prohibition. This safe harbor is intended to avoid chilling good-faith cybersecurity
improvements while still forbidding rogue or malicious intrusions.)
(4) Government-induced viewpoint-based censorship of political speech. —
No officer, employee, or person acting under the direction, control, or authority of the
United States (including any department, agency, instrumentality, or agent thereof), and no
person acting under color of federal law, shall knowingly and intentionally use their official
position or influence to coerce or significantly encourage any private entity (such as a social
media platform, internet search engine, news outlet, or other publisher) to suppress,
remove, label, restrict, or reduce the visibility of lawfully expressed content because of the
viewpoint expressed in that content. Prohibited forms of coercion or inducement include,
but are not limited to:
(A) threatening (or implying) that a private entity will face adverse government
action—such as regulatory scrutiny, enforcement, prosecution, or loss of government
benefits or contracts—if it does not moderate content in a particular way;
(B) exerting persistent official pressure or making repeated directives to a private

platform regarding content moderation (even without an explicit threat), such that the
platform’s independent judgment is overborne and it removes or suppresses content due to
the government’s influence;
(C) deputizing or colluding with a private platform’s employees to achieve censorship
that the government itself could not directly mandate (for example, embedding government
staff within a company’s content moderation team, or otherwise commandeering the
platform’s decision-making processes); or
(D) using any other form of intimidation, control, or undue influence to cause a private
platform or publisher to censor content that it would not have censored but for the
government’s pressure—when the censorship is based on the ideas, opinions, or political
viewpoint expressed.
Nothing in Sec.101(a)(4) prohibits bona fide, factual, lawful notifications by government
actors about content that is not protected by the First Amendment (e.g., true threats,
incitement, or foreign agent activity), provided such communications are informational and
non-coercive.
(Nothing in this paragraph prohibits government officials from engaging in their own
counterspeech—i.e., speaking out to correct misinformation or express disagreement with
content—so long as they do not threaten or coerce others to take down content. Nor does it
prevent officials from notifying platforms about content not protected by the First
Amendment (such as true threats, incitement, or foreign disinformation by covert agents)
provided such notifications are purely informational and carry no element of coercion.
General exhortations to the public or to platforms to be vigilant against misinformation are
not prohibited, so long as they do not cross into targeted pressure to remove specific
viewpoints. This paragraph also does not confer any right on a private platform to disregard
lawful court orders or statutory obligations to remove content (e.g., compliance with valid
copyright takedown orders or foreign agent registration requirements is unaffected). In short,
this provision forbids government officials from achieving indirectly, through unofficial
pressure, what the Constitution forbids them from doing directly. It targets “jawboning” and
de facto commandeering of private moderation decisions, and shall be construed in harmony
with First Amendment principles. )
(5) Discriminatory abuse of governmental processes (“political lawfare”). —
No person acting under color of federal, state, or local law shall initiate or use any official
legal process with the primary purpose of impeding, obstructing, or retaliating against any
person’s campaign for federal office (or their supporters) without a good-faith basis in law
and fact. In other words, it is unlawful to misuse criminal investigations, civil lawsuits,
administrative proceedings, legislative inquiries, or other official processes as a weapon to
burden or undermine a political opponent’s candidacy, absent a legitimate, substantiated
legal justification. Prohibited acts include:

(A) knowingly initiating or pursuing charges, indictments, or investigations against a
candidate mainly to generate negative publicity or tie up the candidate’s time during an
election, where the accusations lack serious evidence of wrongdoing or rely on a pretextual
legal theory;
(B) filing (or causing to be filed) civil suits or administrative complaints—such as
frivolous challenges to ballot access, baseless ethics complaints, or unwarranted campaign
finance investigations—with the intent to harass a candidate or their campaign, rather
than to resolve a genuine dispute, and without any reasonable expectation of success on the
merits;
(C) selectively enforcing laws or regulations (tax laws, permitting rules, professional
licensing, etc.) against a candidate, campaign staffer, donor, or politically active individual
primarily because of their political affiliation or activity, rather than due to a neutral
enforcement trigger; or
(D) using the powers of one’s public office (e.g., subpoena power, audit authority,
legislative investigative powers) in a manner designed to harass or embarrass a political
opponent or their campaign during an election, as opposed to pursuing a legitimate
government purpose.
(This paragraph is not intended to inhibit legitimate law enforcement or regulatory actions
during election seasons. An official proceeding that is supported by probable cause or a
strong factual basis, and that would have been undertaken in the normal course regardless
of the target’s candidacy or politics, is not an unlawful “political” use of process. The burden
is on any plaintiff alleging a violation of this provision to prove by clear and convincing
evidence that the proceeding was initiated without substantial legal or factual foundation
and primarily for a partisan or retaliatory purpose. Courts hearing such claims shall
exercise caution and, where appropriate, defer any ruling until the underlying proceeding (if
still ongoing) has been resolved—so as to avoid undue interference with that
proceeding—unless the plaintiff shows that irreparable harm is imminent and that the lack
of merit is manifest. Nothing in this Act authorizes lawsuits against judges or other neutral
adjudicators for their official rulings (judicial immunity for decision-makers remains
intact); rather, this targets those who instigate sham proceedings or investigations. Likewise,
good-faith enforcement of generally applicable laws (e.g., prosecuting actual corruption or
crime by a candidate) is not prohibited, even if it occurs in an election year, so long as it’s
based on genuine evidence and consistent standards.)
(6) Covert interference with political campaigns and advocacy. —

United States (including any member of the intelligence community, law enforcement, or
the armed forces), and no person acting under color of federal, state, or local law, shall
engage in any undercover, deceptive, or covert operation intended to surveil, infiltrate,
disrupt, or sow discord within:

(A) any campaign for federal office; or
(B) any organization or group of U.S. persons engaged in political expression related to a
federal election—
unless such operation is part of a bona fide law enforcement or intelligence investigation
that meets strict criteria. Specifically, a covert operation is permitted only if:
(i) it is predicated on reasonable suspicion of a specific criminal violation (for law
enforcement) or on a specific foreign threat (for intelligence); and
(ii) it has been authorized and is overseen through established procedures that ensure
compliance with all applicable laws and protect constitutional rights (including obtaining
any required court orders or warrants, and securing high-level approval for sensitive
investigative matters).
Prohibited acts under this paragraph include:
(i) any government agent or informant assuming a false identity or concealing their
affiliation to infiltrate the meetings, communications, or activities of a campaign or political
group, without satisfying the above criteria;
(ii) any government officer or agent collecting or maintaining information about the
membership, meetings, or plans of a domestic political organization without a properly
authorized investigative predicate (for example, querying a government database for
information on donors to a political cause absent a relevant criminal or national security
justification is prohibited); and
(iii) any covert technique or technology used by a government official designed to disrupt
or undermine the internal operations or reputation of a political campaign or group (such as
planting false rumors, instigating factional infighting, deploying obstructive technologies
such as those that jam communications systems, changing or inserting threats onto
candidate websites or encouraging illegal actions by volunteers or staff in order to discredit
the organization).
(This provision bolsters existing First and Fourth Amendment protections. It does not
restrict legitimate investigations of crimes or foreign espionage or influence that incidentally
involve political actors, so long as those investigations strictly adhere to constitutional and
statutory requirements (e.g., obtaining warrants under FISA where required, following DOJ
guidelines for sensitive investigative matters). However, it firmly establishes that agencies
shall not investigate or monitor Americans just because of their political activities absent a
specific indication of unlawful activity or foreign sponsorship. If a federal employee or agent
violates this paragraph, affected parties may seek relief under this Act. Nothing in this
paragraph diminishes the government’s ability to protect candidates and officials through
legitimate security measures (like defensive briefings or monitoring known foreign agents); it
is aimed at preventing recurrences of historical abuses where agencies surveilled or

SEC. 102. Protection of ballot access for qualified federal candidates.
(a) Exclusive federal determination of qualifications. —
No State (including any State officer or any executive, judicial, or legislative body of a
State) shall adjudicate, impose, or enforce any rule of law that has the effect of excluding,
removing, or disqualifying a candidate from the ballot for President, Vice President,
Senator, or Representative in Congress on grounds of alleged “insurrection,” “rebellion,” or
other disqualifying misconduct under Section 3 of the Fourteenth Amendment, except as
expressly authorized by Act of Congress. In plain terms, the determination of whether a
candidate is disqualified from federal office under Section 3 of the 14th Amendment (or any
similar constitutional provision) is reserved to federal authority; states may not unilaterally
bar an otherwise qualified candidate from the ballot for federal office by invoking such
provisions.
(b) Right of action and removal. —
If any state or local official or body seeks to deny ballot access to, or to remove from the
ballot, a candidate for federal office on the grounds of insurrection, rebellion, or similar
political offense (or if any private person initiates a proceeding to do so under state law), the
affected candidate may immediately remove the matter to the appropriate U.S. district

court. Upon such removal, the federal court shall have exclusive jurisdiction to decide the
candidate’s eligibility, applying federal law. The Act hereby confers a federal cause of action
for injunctive and declaratory relief to any candidate whose eligibility for federal office is
challenged or denied on such grounds, allowing them to obtain an expedited ruling on the
merits in federal court. The removing candidate shall file a notice of removal in the state
proceeding and a copy in federal court; the state proceeding shall be stayed and no state

respectfully urged to expedite such appeal given the time-sensitive nature of ballot access

(c) No inference from non-enforcement. —
This section neither mandates nor forbids Congress itself from enforcing Section 3 of the
Fourteenth Amendment via appropriate legislation or proceedings. It simply preempts
unilateral state action in this area. The absence of a current congressional determination of
ineligibility for any given individual shall not be construed by any state as license to decide
the matter on its own. Conversely, if Congress (or a federal court applying federal law) in
the future formally adjudicates that a person is disqualified under Section 3, states shall
give full effect to that determination in their ballot access decisions.
(d) Other candidate qualifications. —
Nothing in this section affects the ability of states to enforce generally applicable ballot
access requirements permissible under federal law (such as age, residency, signature
requirements, filing deadlines, etc., as provided by Article I, §4 and Article II, §1, or related
federal statutes). It is aimed specifically at preventing ad hoc state-level decisions about
disqualifying candidates for federal office based on accusations of insurrection, rebellion, or
similar “unfitness” grounds that lie beyond the state’s purview.
(e) Enforcement. —
A violation of this section by a state official (intentionally keeping a qualified candidate off
the federal ballot in contravention of this section) shall be deemed a violation of Section
101(a)(5) (abuse of official process) and Section 101(a)(8) (material burden on the election)

such suit, the court may issue an injunction placing the candidate on the ballot and may
award damages or other relief as appropriate. Additionally, the Attorney General is
authorized to bring a civil action to enforce this section against any state or local official or
entity.

SEC. 103. Prohibition on private funding of federal election
administration.
(a) Ban on soliciting or using private funds. —
No State or local government (or any subdivision or official thereof) that administers a
federal election may solicit, accept, or use any funds, grants, goods, or in-kind services from
a private entity for the purpose of conducting, supporting, or administering the election
(including voter registration, voting operations, vote counting, or voter education related to
election procedures), except as provided in subsection (b). In essence, all funding for the
administration of federal elections must come from public sources (government
appropriations or the Trust Fund established by this Act), not from private donations, to
avoid real or perceived partisan influence.
(b) Limited exceptions. —
The only exceptions to subsection (a)’s ban are:
(1) the routine use of publicly available facilities or resources that happen to be donated
or rented at low cost, such as a church allowing its hall to be used as a polling place without
charge, or civic volunteers serving as poll workers without pay—provided such customary
contributions are offered broadly and not targeted to influence specific jurisdictions;
(2) any private funds expressly provided by Act of Congress (such as a
congressionally-authorized foundation grant program) and accepted through official
channels; and
(3) goods of de minimis value (under $500 aggregate per election) given as gestures of
community support (for example, a local business sending pizza to poll workers)—such
token contributions must be disclosed and made available equally regardless of jurisdiction.
The Election Assistance Commission (EAC) may by regulation clarify additional minimal
exceptions that do not pose a risk of skewing resources or voter turnout.
(c) Enforcement through civil action. —
If a jurisdiction or official violates subsection (a) by accepting prohibited private funding,
the Department of Justice, any affected candidate, or any political committee or voter in
that jurisdiction may bring a civil action under Title II of this Act to enjoin the practice and
require that any funds received be disgorged to the U.S. Treasury or to an appropriate
public fund. The court shall order prompt cessation of the private-funded activity and
repayment of the funds (or equivalent value) to ensure no net benefit is retained from the
unlawful contribution.
(d) Criminal penalty for willful violation. —
Any election official who knowingly and willfully solicits or accepts private funding in
violation of subsection (a), where the total value is $500 or more, shall be fined not more
than $10,000 or imprisoned not more than 2 years, or both. Likewise, any person (including
a nonprofit or donor) who knowingly offers or provides such funding in order to affect the
administration of a federal election shall be subject to the same penalties. These criminal
Page 17 of 58
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 18 / 58>
Kirk–Evers Free Speech & Democracy Protection Act of 2026 Version: v1.0_2026-09-10
penalties are intended for egregious or knowing violations; inadvertent acceptance of small
private donations that is promptly returned upon discovery may be addressed via civil
enforcement.
(e) Relationship to state laws. —
This section sets a federal floor of protection. State laws that are more stringent in
prohibiting private funding of elections (for example, a state ban on any in-kind donations
or a lower monetary threshold for penalties) are not preempted; states remain free to
impose stricter prohibitions so long as they do not permit what this federal law forbids.
Conversely, if a state law is more permissive than this section (allowing private funding
that this Act would ban), then for any election in which federal offices are on the ballot, this
section’s prohibition controls. In mixed federal-state elections, states may still allow private
funding for the purely state or local portions of the election if clearly segregated, but any
resource that also touches federal contest administration must comply with this section.
(f) Transition and grace period. —
To avoid disrupting ongoing election preparations, any jurisdiction that, on the date of
enactment, is a party to an existing agreement accepting private election administration
funds shall notify the funding provider that such funds will no longer be accepted or used
for federal election purposes. The jurisdiction shall make good-faith efforts to return or
refuse funds not yet expended, or to segregate and not use them for the federal portion of
election costs. The Election Assistance Commission may allow a brief grace period (not to
exceed one election cycle) for jurisdictions to wind down and replace private grants with
public funds, provided that any private funds used during the transition are publicly
disclosed and reported to Congress. By the next federal election after enactment, full
compliance with this section is required in all jurisdictions.

individual to resolve a claim under this Act by arbitration (or prevent them from bringing a
case in court) is unenforceable, unless after the dispute arises the individual specifically
agrees to arbitrate. Similarly, any nondisclosure agreement, confidentiality provision, or
other contractual term that purports to prohibit a party or witness from reporting or
testifying about violations of this Act is unenforceable as contrary to public policy.
Employees or contractors may not be sanctioned for reporting, disclosing, or participating in
investigations or litigation concerning alleged violations of this Act, except to the extent
necessary to protect properly classified national-security information and consistent with
lawful whistleblower channels.
(f) Special rule regarding judicial immunity. —
Nothing in this Act authorizes suing a judge for actions taken in the exercise of judicial
authority (e.g., issuing rulings, injunctions, or sentencing decisions), even if those actions
are alleged to be erroneous or taken with improper motive. Judges retain absolute
immunity from damages for judicial acts. If a person believes a judge improperly interfered
in an election or political process, their remedy lies in appeal, mandamus, or judicial
conduct proceedings, not an action under this Act. However, non-judicial officials who
procure or execute a judge’s order in an unlawful manner may be sued (for example, if a
county official knowingly seeks a court order to remove a candidate from the ballot in
violation of Section 102, that official can be sued under this Act—though the judge who
issued the order cannot). Also, a declaratory judgment may be sought to clarify that a prior
judicial order was unconstitutional or preempted (as occurred when certain state courts
attempted to remove federal candidates from ballots), but in such a case the action is
against the officials implementing or threatening to implement the order, not against the
judge who issued it.

SEC. 202. Remedies, damages, and allocation of awards.
(a) Treble damages for willful violations. —
If the trier of fact (judge or jury) finds that a defendant’s violation of this Act was willful,
deliberate, or committed with knowledge of the law and specific intent to flout it, the court
may, in its discretion, increase the amount of monetary damages (compensatory or
statutory) up to three times the amount of actual damages incurred (or up to three times
the statutory damages that would otherwise be awarded). This “treble damages” provision
is intended to strongly deter especially egregious or flagrant violations by ensuring
wrongdoers face consequences greater than the harm they inflicted. Treble damages under
this subsection are considered punitive in nature and shall not be awarded in addition to
separate punitive damages under Section 201(c)(3)(C); rather, the plaintiff may elect either
an award of treble damages or a separate award of punitive damages for the same conduct.

Recognizing that certain violations of this Act may cause real harms that are difficult to

the Democracy Defense and Remediation Trust Fund established under Title III. This

(2) Court adjustment for cause. —
The court may adjust the percentage allocation between the plaintiff and the Trust Fund

Any funds allocated to a private plaintiff under this subsection must be used by that
plaintiff for activities consistent with the purposes of this Act. A nonprofit organization
receiving such an award shall dedicate the funds to efforts such as voter education, election
integrity initiatives, protecting free speech, legal reform, or other pro-democracy programs
that benefit the public (consistent with its tax-exempt purposes, if applicable). An
individual citizen plaintiff receiving an award shall, as a condition of receipt, either apply
the funds to a public-interest project of their choosing (related to democracy reform) or
donate a substantial portion to a recognized nonprofit engaged in such work.
The court, in its discretion, may require a plan or affidavit from the plaintiff detailing
how their share will be used in the public interest, and can make the payment to the
plaintiff conditional on compliance with that plan. (This ensures the “bounty” portion
directly furthers the Act’s goals and is not misused for purely private benefit – analogous to
cy pres principles in class actions, but mandated by statute here to maintain alignment
with the Act’s purpose.) The court may waive this requirement for an individual plaintiff to
a reasonable extent (or approve an alternative charitable use of the funds) if strict
compliance would impose undue hardship or if the plaintiff’s proposed use, though not
strictly within the Act’s scope, has clear public benefit (the court must report any such
alternative allocation to Congress).

If a case is resolved by settlement, the parties should, wherever feasible, specify the
agreed allocation between the plaintiff and the Trust Fund in the settlement. The default
assumption is the 60/40 split applies unless a different allocation is approved by the court.
Defendants shall not attempt to manipulate the allocation to undermine the plaintiff’s
incentive (for example, a defendant cannot demand that the entire settlement go to the
Trust Fund to deprive the plaintiff organization of resources; any such side arrangement
could be voided by the court as against public policy). The court must review any settlement
in a public-interest suit to ensure the allocation is fair and in keeping with the Act.

An action under this Act must be commenced within 5 years after the date on which the

violations occurring before the date of enactment of this Act, a special retroactive lookback

time-barred under the normal limitations). This one-time lookback aims to address recent
egregious attacks on election integrity and free speech that were not actionable or not
discovered until now. After this window, the normal limitations apply.

SEC. 203. Representative actions by harmed organizations.
(a) Right to bring representative actions. — Except as otherwise limited in this Act, any
qualified public-interest plaintiff (as defined in Section 4(11A)) that has suffered an injury

definable group of persons who suffered harms cognizable under this Act that arose from

representational standing as described in Section 201(b)(2).

(3) proceeding as a representative action is fair and practicable and will permit the court

(A) Organizational or member injury: the plaintiff organization has suffered an

authorize limited cy-pres or structured distributions to identifiable victims only to the
extent feasible and consistent with the remedial plan.
(4) Safeguards against double recovery. The court shall adopt procedures to avoid
duplicate recovery by individual claimants and to ensure that any public-interest plaintiff’s
share is subject to judicial oversight as provided in Section 202.
(d) Limited discovery. Discovery directed to the putative group shall be stayed pending
certification except as the court permits for good cause.

(b) Purpose of the Trial Period. — The Trial Period is intended to permit Congress, the
Judiciary, and relevant oversight bodies to evaluate the real-world operation and

permanently uncapped. In particular, the Trial Period shall provide a limited, controlled

(1) collect empirical data and assess the fiscal, insurance-market, litigation, judicial,

(2) observe and allow courts, the Democracy Defense and Remediation Trust Fund, and

phase-ins, procedural reforms, or other refinements — so that the remedies ultimately
continued beyond the Trial Period are effective, sustainable, and protective of the public
interest.

record necessary for prudent, permanent policy decisions.

SEC. 301. Establishment of Trust Fund.
There is established in the Treasury of the United States a trust fund to be known as the
“Democracy Defense and Remediation Trust Fund” (hereafter the “Trust Fund”). The Trust
Fund shall consist of:
(1) Appropriated funds. —
Such amounts as may be appropriated by Congress to initiate or replenish the fund
(including an initial appropriation of $30,000,000 authorized upon enactment, to remain
available until expended).
(2) Civil recoveries and penalties. —
All amounts of money payable to the United States as a result of judgments, damages, civil
penalties, or settlements under this Act that are designated for the Trust Fund pursuant to
Section 202(c) (typically 35% of awards in public suits, plus any portion allocated by court).
This includes any amounts forfeited by defendants or disgorged as equitable relief that are
directed to the United States or the Trust Fund.
(3) Criminal fines. —
Any criminal fines imposed for offenses established by this Act (Title IV) shall, after any
victim restitution, be deposited into the Trust Fund rather than the general fund of the
Treasury. The same applies to any forfeiture proceeds from criminal cases under this Act
(e.g., illegal gains or instrumentalities seized)—after satisfying restitution, they go to the
Trust Fund.

Title IV — Criminal Offenses and Penalties
SEC. 401. Offense of election subversion.
(a) In general. —
Whoever knowingly and willfully engages in a scheme or concerted course of conduct
(including a conspiracy) that involves multiple violations of law or multiple acts of
interference, with the intent to subvert, nullify, or overturn the free and fair conduct of a
federal election, or to otherwise undermine the actual or apparent expression of voters’ will
in a federal election, shall be guilty of the felony offense of “Election Subversion.” Lawful
political advocacy, protest, assembly, petitioning of government, or contesting of election
results through lawful processes shall not, by itself, constitute an offense under this section.

<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 50 / 58>
Kirk–Evers Free Speech & Democracy Protection Act of 2026 Version: v1.0_2026-09-10
To be liable for Election Subversion, an individual (acting alone or with others) must
commit or attempt at least one overt act that is independently unlawful—e.g., an act that
constitutes voter intimidation, ballot fraud, unauthorized ballot destruction, computer
tampering, or another violation of Title I or other federal or state election laws—as part of a
broader plan or scheme.
The overt acts must be related and not isolated incidents; they must be part of a pattern or
coordinated effort to corrupt the election outcome or punish lawful political participation.
The perpetrator must act with the specific intent to procure an illegitimate advantage or
outcome in a federal election, or to retaliate against or disenfranchise voters, candidates, or
others for participating lawfully—in short, intent to subvert democracy in that election.
(This offense targets orchestrated, multi-faceted schemes to undermine elections—for
example: a coordinated plot to stuff illegal ballots while discarding real ones and spreading
disinformation to cover it up; or a conspiracy among officials to falsely certify results and
intimidate those who object. It is analogous to a “conspiracy against rights” or a RICO
charge, but focused on electoral integrity rather than requiring, say, a protected class or
financial enterprise. It ensures that if various illegal acts (threats, fraud, tampering) are
deployed in combination to overturn an election, they can be charged under one
comprehensive offense to capture the gravity of the scheme. If all acts were somehow legal
individually, then no “unlawful overt act” exists and this crime would not apply—i.e.,
partisan but legal tactics like gerrymandering are not criminalized by this, only schemes
involving illegal acts.)
(b) Penalties. —
A person convicted of Election Subversion shall be subject to the following penalties, in
addition to any penalties for the underlying acts themselves (which may be charged
separately):
(1) Basic penalty. —
A fine of up to $500,000, and/or imprisonment for up to 15 years.
(2) Enhanced penalty—outcome change. —
If the offense actually changed the officially reported outcome of a federal election (for
example, the scheme succeeded in causing the wrong candidate to be declared winner), or if
it was so extensive it could have changed the outcome of a reasonably close election (even if
ultimately caught or reversed), then the maximum imprisonment is increased to 20 years.
(3) Enhanced penalty—violence. —
If the offense involved violence or attempted violence—such as kidnapping, attempted
murder, or inflicting serious bodily injury in the course of the scheme (e.g., an attack on an
election official or bombing of a ballot storage facility)—then the defendant may be
imprisoned for up to 25 years. (This is in addition to any punishment for the violent acts

Kirk–Evers Free Speech & Democracy Protection Act of 2026 Version: v1.0_2026-09-10
themselves; e.g., attempted murder charges. The 25-year cap is just for the subversion

(4) Disqualification from office. —
In addition to imprisonment and fines, the court may order that any defendant who is
convicted of Election Subversion shall be disqualified from holding any office of honor, trust,
or profit under the United States. In particularly egregious cases (e.g., where the offender
was a public official betraying their oath), the court may impose such disqualification for a

envisioned by Section 3 of the 14th Amendment through the criminal process, allowing a
judge to bar the worst offenders from future governmental authority.
(c) Forfeiture. —
Whoever is convicted of Election Subversion shall forfeit to the United States any
property which constitutes or is derived from proceeds traceable to the offense, and any
property used or intended to be used to commit the offense. For example, if conspirators
raised funds to finance the scheme or received bribes, those funds can be forfeited, and if
equipment (computers, devices) were used, they can be seized. Proceeds from forfeiture,
after restitution to identifiable victims, shall be deposited in the Trust Fund. The forfeiture
process shall follow 18 U.S.C. §§ 981–982, with the court making necessary findings
post-conviction.
SEC. 402. Penalties for officials abusing authority.
This section provides additional penalties and consequences specifically aimed at public
officials who abuse their authority to interfere in elections or related political rights, beyond
the general criminal penalties:
(a) Federal officials—removal and employment bar. —
Any federal officer or employee who is convicted of any felony under this Act (including the
Election Subversion offense or other offenses in this Title, or related federal offenses
committed to carry out a violation of this Act) shall, upon conviction, forfeit their office or
employment. If they have not already been removed, conviction under this Act shall be
deemed cause for immediate removal. Furthermore, no person who is convicted of such an
offense shall be employed by the United States government (in any civilian or military
capacity) for a minimum of 5 years following the completion of their sentence. The court
may extend this federal employment bar up to a lifetime ban in extreme cases, by explicit
provision at sentencing. (This ensures, for instance, that a federal agent or official who
corrupts an election cannot remain in or return to government service for a substantial
period, if ever.)
(b) State and local officials—notification and encouragement of removal. —
If a state or local official is convicted in federal court of a crime under this Act, the Attorney

SEC. 403. Penalties for ballot fraud and tampering.
While many acts of ballot fraud and tampering are already federal crimes under title 52
(e.g., 52 U.S.C. § 20511) or title 18 (e.g., 18 U.S.C. §§ 241, 242, etc.), Congress finds it
necessary to enhance penalties for large-scale or conspiratorial election fraud to
appropriately punish and deter such conduct:
(a) Large-scale fraud enhancement. —
If any person is convicted under any federal law for fraudulently casting ballots, counting
false votes, bribery in voting, or similar offenses in a federal election, and the offense
involved 10 or more fraudulent ballots or votes (or if fewer, but the conduct was part of a
scheme to produce multiple illegal votes), then notwithstanding any lesser penalty in the
underlying statute, the offender shall be fined and imprisoned for up to 10 years. If the
offense involved 100 or more fraudulent ballots or votes (or could have altered the outcome
of a federal contest), the maximum imprisonment shall be 15 years. These enhanced
maximums apply unless the underlying law already provides equal or higher penalties.
(b) Tampering with voting machines or tabulation. —
Any person who, without authorization, tampers with or attempts to manipulate a voting
Page 53 of 58
<PARSED TEXT FOR PAGE: 54 / 58>
Kirk–Evers Free Speech & Democracy Protection Act of 2026 Version: v1.0_2026-09-10
machine, tabulator, or election results (by electronic means or otherwise) in a federal
election shall be fined or imprisoned for up to 10 years, or both. If such tampering involves
multiple machines or is done by an election official abusing their trust, the maximum shall
be 15 years. (This provision complements Section 101(a)(3) by adding explicit criminal
penalty; many such acts also violate 18 U.S.C. § 1030 or other statutes, which can be
charged as well.)
(c) Voter intimidation. —
Existing law (18 U.S.C. § 594) punishes voter intimidation with up to 1 year imprisonment.
To strengthen deterrence, if the intimidation is widespread or involves the threat of
violence, the penalty shall be up to 5 years imprisonment. The Attorney General shall
update charging guidelines to seek felony charges (e.g., under civil rights statutes) for
serious intimidation cases, and not only misdemeanor charges.
(d) Concurrent charges. —
A prosecution under Section 401 (Election Subversion) shall not preclude separate charges
for specific acts of fraud, intimidation, computer crime, or similar offenses. Conversely, a
person may be charged with stand-alone offenses (ballot fraud, etc.) without the broader
subversion charge if their conduct, while illegal, did not rise to a concerted scheme at the
scale of Section 401. The intent is to give prosecutors flexibility to charge according to the
gravity and coordination of the conduct.
(e) No double jeopardy issue. —
If conduct violates both this Act and other federal criminal statutes, prosecution may
proceed under both so long as each requires proof of a fact the other does not (or they
address distinct societal harms). However, sentencing courts should avoid “double counting”
the same harm under multiple enhancements.
(Congressional finding: These graduated penalties reflect that small-scale ballot fraud is
serious but a coordinated scheme involving many ballots is vastly more dangerous to
democracy and thus warrants harsher punishment. The approach parallels some election
laws that scale penalties by volume of fraud.)

SEC. 504. Severability.
If any provision of this Act, or the application of any provision to any person or
circumstance, is held to be invalid or unconstitutional, the remainder of the Act and the
application of its provisions to other persons or circumstances shall not be affected. The
provisions of this Act are severable. Congress declares that it would have enacted this Act
and each provision, section, sentence, and clause thereof, irrespective of the fact that any
one or more parts may be declared invalid.
In particular, if any defendant or class of defendants (for example, state officials) is found
immune from suit or not subject to a provision, the provisions shall remain enforceable
against others (e.g., individuals or the federal government). If any remedy or penalty is
judged excessive in a specific case, the court should enforce it to the maximum allowed
extent rather than voiding the provision entirely (e.g., if a certain damages cap is required,
impose the cap but do not nullify the cause of action). Courts are instructed to construe the
Act to uphold its validity to the fullest possible degree, and to sever only what must be
severed, preserving the remainder.
(Congress stresses: even if parts of this Act fall, the resolve to protect free speech and fair
elections stands—the Act’s provisions should be implemented to the greatest extent
permissible, to ensure no violation goes unaddressed even if a novel legal theory fails.)
